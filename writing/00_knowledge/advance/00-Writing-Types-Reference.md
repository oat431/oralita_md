# Writing Types — Reference

> Advanced reference tier. For the practical map and the how-to guides, start with [[00-Writing-Types]] and the numbered guides in the fundamental tier.
>
> **Structure:** this reference expands into three tiers — [[tier-1-modes/overview|Tier 1 · Rhetorical Modes]], [[tier-2-domains/overview|Tier 2 · Writing Domains]], [[tier-3-craft/overview|Tier 3 · Craft and Process]].

## Overview

Writing is the deliberate use of language to communicate ideas, information, experiences, or arguments to a particular audience.

A **writing type** is not determined only by its format. It emerges from several interacting dimensions:

- **Purpose:** What should the writing accomplish?
- **Audience:** Who will read it, and what do they already know?
- **Content:** What information, ideas, or experiences must be communicated?
- **Context:** Where, when, and under what conditions will it be read?
- **Medium:** Will it appear in a book, report, email, website, application, or another channel?
- **Convention:** What structural and stylistic expectations apply?

Most texts are **hybrid**. For example, a technical proposal may explain a system, analyze alternatives, and persuade decision-makers within the same document.

---

## 1. A Multidimensional Model of Writing

The term *writing type* often combines concepts that should be distinguished.

| Dimension | Central question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Rhetorical mode | How does the text develop its ideas? | Description, narration, exposition, argumentation |
| Communicative purpose | What should the text accomplish? | Inform, explain, instruct, persuade, document, entertain |
| Genre | What recognizable form does the text follow? | Essay, report, tutorial, proposal, novel |
| Domain | In what professional or social context is it used? | Academic, technical, business, journalistic |
| Medium | Through which channel is it delivered? | Print, email, website, social media |
| Audience | For whom is it written? | General readers, experts, customers, managers |

This distinction prevents a common classification error. For example, an **academic article** is a genre within an academic domain, while **argumentation** is a rhetorical mode that may appear in academic articles, business proposals, opinion columns, and many other genres.

---

## 2. The Rhetorical Situation

Effective writing responds to a rhetorical situation consisting of five primary elements:

1. **Writer:** The person or organization communicating the message.
2. **Purpose:** The intended outcome of the communication.
3. **Audience:** The readers whose knowledge, needs, and expectations shape the text.
4. **Message:** The ideas, evidence, or experience being communicated.
5. **Context:** The circumstances in which the communication occurs.

A useful planning formula is:

> **Effective writing = appropriate content + appropriate structure + appropriate language for a specific audience and purpose**

Before writing, answer the following questions:

- What should readers know, believe, feel, or do after reading?
- What do readers already know about the subject?
- What evidence or explanation will they require?
- What objections, risks, or misunderstandings are likely?
- What tone and level of detail are appropriate?
- What constraints apply to length, format, time, or publication?

## 3. Core Rhetorical Modes

### 3.1 Descriptive Writing

**Purpose:** To represent a person, place, object, event, process, or condition through precise details.

Descriptive writing helps readers perceive or understand a subject. It may use sensory language, spatial organization, technical attributes, or carefully selected observations.

**Characteristics:**

- Precise nouns and verbs
- Relevant sensory or technical details
- Clear spatial or logical organization
- Consistent point of view
- Selective rather than exhaustive detail
- Figurative language when appropriate

**Common structures:**

- General impression to specific details
- Foreground to background
- Left to right or top to bottom
- External appearance to internal characteristics
- Most important to least important feature

**Common forms:**

- Product descriptions
- Setting descriptions
- Character profiles
- Observation notes
- Scientific descriptions
- Incident and condition reports

**Example:**

> The server room remained at 24°C, but the eastern rack emitted a continuous high-frequency sound. Two warning indicators on the upper power unit flashed amber at five-second intervals.

**Quality criterion:** The details should contribute to the reader’s understanding rather than merely decorate the text.

---

### 3.2 Narrative Writing

**Purpose:** To present events or experiences as a meaningful sequence.

Narrative writing connects events through time, causality, conflict, and change. It may be factual or fictional.

**Characteristics:**

- A clear sequence of events
- Participants or characters
- A setting
- A point of view
- Conflict, tension, or a central problem
- Change, discovery, or resolution

**Basic narrative structure:**

1. **Orientation:** Introduce the setting, participants, and initial situation.
2. **Complication:** Present a problem, conflict, or disruption.
3. **Development:** Show actions, consequences, and increasing tension.
4. **Climax:** Reach the decisive moment.
5. **Resolution:** Explain the outcome.
6. **Reflection:** Clarify the meaning or significance of the experience.

**Common forms:**

- Short stories
- Novels
- Memoirs
- Case studies
- Historical accounts
- User stories
- Incident timelines
- Personal essays

**Example:**

> At 09:15, the monitoring system reported a sudden increase in failed requests. The operations team initially suspected a network fault, but the failure continued after traffic was rerouted. At 09:42, an engineer identified an incompatible database migration as the cause.

**Quality criterion:** Events should form a meaningful causal sequence, not merely a chronological list.

**Related guide:** [[02-Storytelling-Guide]]

---

### 3.3 Expository Writing

**Purpose:** To inform, define, clarify, or explain a subject.

Expository writing emphasizes accuracy, organization, and comprehension. It generally relies on definitions, examples, classifications, comparisons, and causal explanations.

**Characteristics:**

- A clearly defined topic
- Logical organization
- Accurate terminology
- Relevant facts and examples
- Explicit relationships between ideas
- Limited unsupported opinion
- A tone appropriate to the audience

**Common organizational patterns:**

| Pattern | Function |
|---|---|
| Definition | Explains the meaning and boundaries of a concept |
| Classification | Groups items according to shared characteristics |
| Comparison and contrast | Examines similarities and differences |
| Cause and effect | Explains why something occurs and what follows |
| Process explanation | Explains how something works |
| Problem and solution | Defines a problem and evaluates responses |
| General to specific | Introduces a concept before presenting details |
| Known to unknown | Connects unfamiliar ideas to existing knowledge |

**Common forms:**

- Textbooks
- Encyclopedic articles
- Explanatory essays
- Frequently asked questions
- Background sections
- Knowledge-base articles
- Concept documentation

**Example:**

> Version control is a system for recording changes to files over time. It allows contributors to examine previous versions, compare modifications, and coordinate work without manually maintaining multiple copies.

**Quality criterion:** Readers should understand the subject more accurately after reading than they did before.

**Related guide:** [[06-Expository-Writing-Guide]]

---

### 3.4 Analytical Writing

**Purpose:** To examine a subject by separating it into components and interpreting the relationships among them.

Analysis goes beyond presenting information. It identifies patterns, applies criteria, compares alternatives, evaluates causes, or explains significance.

**Characteristics:**

- A focused analytical question
- Explicit criteria or conceptual framework
- Separation of evidence from interpretation
- Identification of patterns and relationships
- Consideration of alternative explanations
- A conclusion derived from the analysis

**Typical analytical sequence:**

1. Define the question or problem.
2. Establish the scope and evaluation criteria.
3. Present relevant evidence.
4. Divide the subject into meaningful components.
5. Compare, interpret, or evaluate those components.
6. Address limitations or alternative interpretations.
7. State the conclusion and its implications.

**Common forms:**

- Literary analysis
- Data analysis
- Root-cause analysis
- Policy analysis
- Comparative reviews
- Research discussions
- System evaluations

**Example:**

> Although response time improved after caching was introduced, the error rate remained unchanged. This pattern suggests that processing capacity was a secondary constraint rather than the primary cause of failure.

**Quality criterion:** The conclusion must follow logically from the evidence and stated criteria.

---

### 3.5 Argumentative Writing

**Purpose:** To establish that a claim is reasonable or preferable by using evidence and logical reasoning.

An argument is not simply a disagreement. It is a structured relationship among claims, reasons, evidence, assumptions, and responses to alternative positions.

**Core components:**

- **Claim:** The proposition the writer wants readers to accept.
- **Reason:** The explanation supporting the claim.
- **Evidence:** The information used to substantiate the reason.
- **Warrant:** The principle connecting the evidence to the claim.
- **Qualification:** The conditions or limits under which the claim applies.
- **Counterargument:** A credible alternative or objection.
- **Rebuttal:** The response to that counterargument.

**Basic structure:**

1. Introduce the issue.
2. State a clear and qualified thesis.
3. Define important terms and assumptions.
4. Present reasons and supporting evidence.
5. Address credible counterarguments.
6. Explain why the evidence supports the conclusion.
7. State the implications or recommended position.

**Example:**

> The organization should introduce automated accessibility testing because early detection reduces remediation costs and prevents recurring interface defects. Automated testing cannot replace manual evaluation, but it can identify common violations during development.

**Quality criterion:** The strength of an argument depends on the quality of its evidence and reasoning, not the confidence of its language.

**Related guide:** [[07-Persuasive-Writing-Guide]]

---

### 3.6 Persuasive Writing

**Purpose:** To influence readers’ beliefs, attitudes, decisions, or actions.

Persuasion may include logical argument, but it also considers credibility, emotion, values, timing, and the cost of action.

Classical rhetoric identifies three major persuasive appeals:

- **Logos:** Reasoning, evidence, and internal consistency
- **Ethos:** The writer’s credibility, expertise, and fairness
- **Pathos:** Emotion, identity, values, and human consequences

**Argumentation and persuasion are related but not identical:**

| Argumentation | Persuasion |
|---|---|
| Seeks rational acceptance of a claim | Seeks a change in belief, attitude, or action |
| Emphasizes evidence and reasoning | Uses reasoning, credibility, emotion, and framing |
| May conclude without a call to action | Often includes a specific call to action |
| Evaluated mainly by logical strength | Also evaluated by audience response and ethics |

**Common forms:**

- Proposals
- Recommendation reports
- Advertisements
- Campaign messages
- Cover letters
- Opinion articles
- Calls to action

**Ethical persuasion should:**

- Represent evidence accurately
- Avoid hiding material risks
- Distinguish facts from assumptions
- Avoid exploiting fear or prejudice
- Present alternatives fairly
- Make the requested action explicit

**Quality criterion:** Persuasion should influence readers without misleading or manipulating them.

**Related guide:** [[07-Persuasive-Writing-Guide]]

---

### 3.7 Instructional Writing

**Purpose:** To enable readers to complete a task safely and successfully.

Instructional writing is action-oriented. Its effectiveness is measured by whether the intended user can produce the expected result.

**Characteristics:**

- A clearly stated outcome
- Defined prerequisites
- Sequential, executable steps
- Imperative verbs
- One primary action per step
- Necessary warnings before risky actions
- Expected results and verification criteria
- Troubleshooting guidance when appropriate

**Recommended structure:**

1. Objective
2. Audience
3. Prerequisites
4. Required tools or materials
5. Procedure
6. Expected result
7. Verification
8. Troubleshooting
9. Recovery or rollback

**Example:**

1. Open the repository directory.
2. Create a new branch from the latest `main` branch.
3. Edit the required Markdown file.
4. Run the documentation checks.
5. Review the generated output before committing the change.

**Quality criterion:** A qualified reader should be able to complete the task without relying on undocumented assumptions.

---

### 3.8 Reflective Writing

**Purpose:** To examine an experience, decision, or learning process and derive meaning from it.

Reflection is more than personal description. It connects experience with interpretation, concepts, and future action.

**A useful reflective structure:**

1. **Experience:** What happened?
2. **Response:** What did I think, feel, or assume?
3. **Analysis:** Why did it happen, and what concepts explain it?
4. **Evaluation:** What worked, failed, or remained uncertain?
5. **Learning:** What did I discover?
6. **Action:** What will I do differently next time?

**Common forms:**

- Learning journals
- Retrospectives
- Professional development records
- Reflective essays
- Post-project reviews
- Decision logs

**Quality criterion:** Reflection should transform experience into transferable learning.

---

## 4. Major Writing Domains

A writing domain is a social or professional context with its own objectives, conventions, evidence standards, and vocabulary.

### 4.1 Academic Writing

**Purpose:** To develop, test, communicate, or evaluate knowledge.

**Characteristics:**

- A precise research question or thesis
- Engagement with existing scholarship
- Evidence-based reasoning
- Explicit research methods when applicable
- Accurate citation and attribution
- Recognition of limitations
- Formal and disciplined language

**Common forms:**

- Essays
- Research papers
- Literature reviews
- Theses and dissertations
- Conference papers
- Critical analyses
- Laboratory reports

Academic writing is not defined merely by complex vocabulary. Its defining qualities are methodological transparency, evidence, traceability, and participation in scholarly discussion.

---

### 4.2 Technical Writing

**Purpose:** To communicate specialized information so that readers can understand a system, make a decision, or perform a task.

**Characteristics:**

- Accuracy and consistency
- Defined terminology
- Task- or decision-oriented organization
- Explicit assumptions and constraints
- Diagrams, tables, examples, or code where useful
- Attention to safety, accessibility, and maintainability
- Content designed for scanning and retrieval

**Common forms:**

- API documentation
- System specifications
- Architecture decision records
- Installation guides
- Runbooks
- Release notes
- Troubleshooting guides
- Technical reports

Technical writing should reduce the reader’s uncertainty and cognitive load.

---

### 4.3 Business and Professional Writing

**Purpose:** To support organizational communication, coordination, decisions, and accountability.

**Characteristics:**

- The main point appears early
- Responsibilities and requested actions are explicit
- Information is concise and operationally relevant
- Risks, costs, deadlines, and dependencies are visible
- Tone reflects the organizational relationship
- Decisions can be traced to evidence and ownership

**Common forms:**

- Emails
- Memos
- Proposals
- Business cases
- Meeting records
- Project reports
- Policies
- Executive summaries

A useful pattern is **BLUF—Bottom Line Up Front**:

1. State the conclusion or request.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Provide supporting evidence.
4. Define the required action, owner, and deadline.

---

### 4.4 Journalistic Writing

**Purpose:** To report and explain matters of public interest.

**Characteristics:**

- Verification of factual claims
- Clear attribution
- Separation of reporting from opinion
- Timeliness and relevance
- Multiple credible perspectives
- Attention to public consequences
- Corrections when errors are discovered

**Common forms:**

- News reports
- Features
- Interviews
- Investigative reports
- Profiles
- Editorials
- Reviews

News reports often use the **inverted pyramid**:

1. Most important information
2. Essential supporting details
3. Context and background
4. Secondary information

**Related guide:** [[06-Expository-Writing-Guide]]

---

### 4.5 Creative Writing

**Purpose:** To create an aesthetic, imaginative, emotional, or interpretive experience.

**Characteristics:**

- Deliberate use of voice and style
- Imagery and figurative language
- Character, setting, conflict, or theme
- Attention to rhythm and sound
- Productive ambiguity
- Original arrangement of language and experience

**Common forms:**

- Fiction
- Poetry
- Drama
- Creative nonfiction
- Screenplays
- Literary memoirs

Creative writing may use any rhetorical mode. Its distinction lies primarily in artistic intention and execution.

**Related guides:** [[02-Storytelling-Guide]], [[03-Media-Script-Guide]]

---

### 4.6 Personal and Social Writing

**Purpose:** To express identity, maintain relationships, record experiences, or participate in communities.

**Common forms:**

- Personal letters
- Diaries
- Social posts
- Messages
- Personal statements
- Biographical notes

The appropriate tone depends strongly on the relationship between writer and reader. Personal writing may be informal, but it should still consider privacy, permanence, and the risk of misinterpretation.

**Related guide:** [[04-Wishing-Note-Guide]]

## 6. Choosing the Appropriate Writing Type

Start with the outcome expected from the reader.

| Desired reader outcome | Primary writing type | Supporting modes |
|---|---|---|
| Understand a concept | Expository | Descriptive, analytical |
| Visualize a subject | Descriptive | Narrative |
| Follow a sequence of events | Narrative | Descriptive, analytical |
| Complete a task | Instructional | Expository, descriptive |
| Accept a conclusion | Argumentative | Analytical, expository |
| Take an action | Persuasive | Argumentative, narrative |
| Compare alternatives | Analytical | Expository, argumentative |
| Learn from an experience | Reflective | Narrative, analytical |
| Make an organizational decision | Business or technical | Analytical, persuasive |
| Experience a story or emotion | Creative | Narrative, descriptive |

Use the following decision sequence:

1. Define the required reader outcome.
2. Identify the audience and its existing knowledge.
3. Select the genre expected in the context.
4. Choose the primary rhetorical mode.
5. Add supporting modes where necessary.
6. Determine the required evidence.
7. Select an appropriate structure, tone, and level of detail.
8. Test whether the text enables the intended outcome.

## 7. Combining Writing Types

Complex documents usually combine multiple modes.

### Example: Technical Proposal

A technical proposal may contain:

- **Exposition** to explain the current system
- **Description** to define the proposed architecture
- **Analysis** to compare available options
- **Argumentation** to justify the recommendation
- **Persuasion** to request approval or funding
- **Instruction** to describe the implementation plan

The writer should still identify one **dominant purpose**. Supporting sections should contribute to that purpose rather than compete with it.

## 8. Structure at the Document Level

Most effective nonfiction documents follow a three-part structure.

### 8.1 Introduction

The introduction should establish:

- The topic
- The purpose
- The audience’s reason to care
- The scope
- The controlling question or thesis
- The organization of the document when necessary

### 8.2 Body

The body should:

- Divide the subject into logical sections
- Place related ideas together
- Present evidence near the relevant claim
- Use headings that communicate meaning
- Make transitions between sections explicit
- Progress from known to unknown or simple to complex

### 8.3 Conclusion

The conclusion should:

- Synthesize the main findings
- Answer the central question
- State implications
- Identify limitations where relevant
- Define the next action when required

A conclusion should not merely repeat the introduction. It should show what follows from the material already presented.

---

## 9. Structure at the Paragraph Level

A functional paragraph usually contains four elements:

1. **Topic sentence:** States the paragraph’s controlling idea.
2. **Development:** Explains or qualifies that idea.
3. **Evidence:** Provides facts, examples, data, or reasoning.
4. **Connection:** Relates the paragraph to the broader argument or next idea.

A useful model is **Point–Evidence–Explanation–Link**:

- **Point:** What does this paragraph claim?
- **Evidence:** What supports the claim?
- **Explanation:** Why does the evidence matter?
- **Link:** How does it connect to the document’s purpose?

Each paragraph should normally perform one primary rhetorical function.

## 10. Evidence and Source Use

Different claims require different forms of support.

| Claim type | Suitable support |
|---|---|
| Factual claim | Verified data, records, or authoritative sources |
| Causal claim | Controlled evidence, mechanism, or comparative analysis |
| Interpretive claim | Textual evidence and a coherent analytical framework |
| Predictive claim | Historical patterns, models, assumptions, and uncertainty |
| Evaluative claim | Explicit criteria and comparative evidence |
| Recommendation | Evidence of benefits, costs, risks, and feasibility |
| Personal reflection | Specific experience connected to analysis |

Good evidence should be:

- Relevant to the claim
- Sufficient for the strength of the conclusion
- Credible and traceable
- Representative rather than selectively chosen
- Current enough for the subject
- Interpreted rather than merely inserted

Always distinguish among:

- **Fact:** A claim supported by verifiable evidence
- **Inference:** A conclusion derived from evidence
- **Assumption:** A proposition accepted without direct verification
- **Opinion:** A judgment or preference
- **Hypothesis:** A testable proposed explanation
- **Recommendation:** A proposed course of action

## 11. Tone, Voice, and Style

### Tone

Tone is the writer’s attitude toward the subject and audience.

Common tones include:

- Formal
- Neutral
- Analytical
- Instructional
- Conversational
- Reflective
- Urgent
- Persuasive

Tone should be selected intentionally rather than produced accidentally.

### Voice

Voice is the recognizable character of the writing. It emerges from vocabulary, sentence structure, rhythm, perspective, and degree of formality.

### Active and Passive Voice

Use active voice when the actor and responsibility matter:

> The operations team restarted the service.

Use passive voice when the action or result is more important than the actor, or when the actor is unknown:

> The service was restarted at 14:30.

Passive voice is not inherently incorrect. The relevant question is whether the sentence makes responsibility and meaning sufficiently clear.

## 12. Clarity Principles

Clear writing reduces unnecessary cognitive effort.

### Prefer:

- Concrete nouns
- Precise verbs
- Defined technical terms
- Short sentences for critical instructions
- Explicit logical relationships
- Consistent terminology
- Examples after abstract explanations
- Headings that describe section content

### Avoid:

- Unnecessary jargon
- Ambiguous pronouns
- Hidden assumptions
- Excessive nominalization
- Repeated qualifications
- Long introductory phrases
- Decorative complexity
- Unexplained abbreviations
- Multiple terms for the same concept

Compare:

> The implementation of the configuration modification was performed by the administrator.

With:

> The administrator changed the configuration.

The second version is shorter, clearer, and more direct.

## 13. The Writing Process

Writing is an iterative process rather than a single act.

### 13.1 Analyze

- Define the purpose.
- Identify the audience.
- Clarify the required outcome.
- Determine constraints.
- Select the genre and primary mode.

### 13.2 Research

- Gather relevant information.
- Evaluate source credibility.
- Record source details.
- Separate evidence from assumptions.
- Identify gaps and conflicting information.

### 13.3 Organize

- Formulate the central question or thesis.
- Group related ideas.
- Select an organizational pattern.
- Create an outline.
- Determine where evidence belongs.

### 13.4 Draft

- Write for completeness before polishing every sentence.
- Keep each section aligned with its purpose.
- Use placeholders for unresolved details.
- Maintain consistent terminology.
- Make assumptions visible.

### 13.5 Revise

Revision addresses meaning and structure:

- Does the text achieve its purpose?
- Is the central claim clear?
- Is the organization logical?
- Is important information missing?
- Are sections redundant?
- Does the evidence support the conclusions?
- Are counterarguments treated fairly?

### 13.6 Edit

Editing addresses expression:

- Improve sentence clarity.
- Remove unnecessary words.
- Correct grammar and punctuation.
- Standardize terminology.
- Check transitions.
- Adjust tone and level of detail.

### 13.7 Proofread and Validate

- Check spelling and formatting.
- Verify names, dates, figures, and references.
- Test instructions where possible.
- Check links and cross-references.
- Confirm accessibility.
- Review the final rendered output.

## 14. Common Writing Problems

| Problem | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| No clear purpose | The text lacks direction | Define the required reader outcome |
| Undefined audience | Detail and tone become inappropriate | Create a reader profile |
| Weak thesis | Readers cannot identify the main claim | State a specific, defensible position |
| Information without structure | Readers cannot form a mental model | Group and sequence related ideas |
| Claims without evidence | Conclusions appear unsupported | Add credible and relevant support |
| Evidence without explanation | Readers must infer its significance | Explain how evidence supports the claim |
| Excessive abstraction | Meaning becomes difficult to visualize | Add concrete examples |
| Unnecessary complexity | Reading requires excessive effort | Simplify syntax and vocabulary |
| Mixed terminology | Readers assume false distinctions | Use one term consistently |
| Hidden assumptions | Reasoning appears incomplete | State relevant assumptions explicitly |
| Repetition | Important points become diluted | Consolidate overlapping sections |
| Premature detail | Readers lose the conceptual overview | Explain the whole before the parts |

## 15. Evaluation Rubric

A piece of writing can be evaluated across the following dimensions.

| Dimension | Evaluation question |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Does the text achieve its intended outcome? |
| Audience | Is it appropriate for the readers’ knowledge and needs? |
| Accuracy | Are factual and technical claims correct? |
| Relevance | Does each section contribute to the purpose? |
| Structure | Are ideas organized into a logical progression? |
| Reasoning | Do conclusions follow from evidence and assumptions? |
| Evidence | Are claims supported by credible and sufficient evidence? |
| Clarity | Can readers understand the text without unnecessary effort? |
| Precision | Are terms, quantities, and relationships stated exactly? |
| Coherence | Do sentences, paragraphs, and sections connect meaningfully? |
| Concision | Does the text avoid unnecessary material? |
| Tone | Is the writer’s attitude appropriate to the context? |
| Usability | Can readers find and apply the required information? |
| Accessibility | Can people with different needs access the content? |
| Correctness | Are grammar, punctuation, formatting, and references correct? |

A practical scoring scale may use:

- **4 — Excellent:** Fully effective; only minor improvements are possible.
- **3 — Competent:** Effective overall but contains identifiable weaknesses.
- **2 — Developing:** Partially effective; substantial revision is required.
- **1 — Inadequate:** Does not yet fulfill its primary purpose.

## 16. Same Topic, Different Writing Types

Consider the topic of remote work.

### Descriptive

> The home office contains a height-adjustable desk, two monitors, a directional microphone, and a window positioned to the left of the screen.

### Narrative

> During the first week of remote work, the team reproduced its office schedule online. After several days of continuous meetings, members began replacing status calls with written updates.

### Expository

> Remote work is an arrangement in which employees perform their duties outside a centralized workplace, typically using digital communication and collaboration systems.

### Analytical

> Productivity increased for individual tasks but declined for work requiring rapid cross-team coordination. The results indicate that task interdependence, rather than physical location alone, influenced performance.

### Argumentative

> The organization should adopt a hybrid policy because it preserves focused work while maintaining regular opportunities for complex in-person collaboration.

### Persuasive

> Approve the six-month hybrid-work trial to reduce office costs, improve employee retention, and collect evidence before introducing a permanent policy.

### Instructional

> Connect to the corporate network, open the team workspace, review the daily priorities, and record any blockers before beginning assigned tasks.

### Reflective

> I initially interpreted fewer meetings as weaker collaboration. After reviewing the project outcomes, I realized that written decision records had made responsibilities clearer.

## 18. Final Checklist

Before publishing, confirm that:

- [ ] The purpose is explicit.
- [ ] The intended audience is identifiable.
- [ ] The genre and rhetorical mode suit the context.
- [ ] The main claim or outcome appears early enough.
- [ ] Each section contributes to the central purpose.
- [ ] Important terms are defined.
- [ ] Claims are supported by appropriate evidence.
- [ ] Facts, assumptions, opinions, and recommendations are distinguishable.
- [ ] Counterarguments or alternatives are addressed where necessary.
- [ ] Paragraphs have clear controlling ideas.
- [ ] Transitions explain relationships between ideas.
- [ ] Terminology is consistent.
- [ ] Sentences are concise and unambiguous.
- [ ] Instructions are complete and executable.
- [ ] Names, dates, figures, links, and references are verified.
- [ ] The final format is readable and accessible.
- [ ] The conclusion answers the central question or defines the next action.

## Summary

Writing types are best understood as combinations of **purpose, rhetorical mode, genre, domain, audience, and medium**.

The core rhetorical modes are:

- **Descriptive:** represents characteristics or conditions
- **Narrative:** organizes events into a meaningful sequence
- **Expository:** explains or informs
- **Analytical:** examines parts, relationships, and significance
- **Argumentative:** supports a claim through evidence and reasoning
- **Persuasive:** influences belief or action
- **Instructional:** enables task completion
- **Reflective:** converts experience into learning

Effective writers do not select a type mechanically. They analyze the rhetorical situation, choose a dominant purpose, combine appropriate modes, and revise the result according to evidence, clarity, structure, and reader needs.
