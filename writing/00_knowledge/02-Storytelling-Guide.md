# Storytelling Guide

> Story structures are a vocabulary for diagnosing stories, not formulas that make every story identical — the way knowing chord progressions doesn't make every song sound the same. Up to parent map [[00-Writing-Types]].

## What Makes a Story (before any structure)

A story = **a character who wants something, meets obstacles, and is changed by the struggle.** Strip everything else away and check these five parts exist:

| Part | Question it answers |
|------|---------------------|
| Character | Who do I follow? |
| Want / Goal | What do they chase? (external) and what do they *need*? (internal — often in conflict with the want) |
| Conflict | What stands in the way? |
| Stakes | What happens if they fail? |
| Change | How are they different at the end? |

No change → not a story, just a sequence of events (fine for logs, deadly for fiction).

## The 8 Story Structures

### 1. Three-Act Structure — the baseline
**Beats:** Act 1 Setup (world + inciting incident + commitment) → Act 2 Confrontation (escalating obstacles, midpoint raises stakes, all-is-lost) → Act 3 Resolution (final confrontation, new status quo).
**Best for:** Any genre, first outlines. **Weakness:** Act Two is enormous and vague — "escalating obstacles for 60 pages" isn't actionable. Most other structures exist to fix this.

### 2. Freytag's Pyramid — the tragedy shape
**Beats:** Exposition → Rising action → **Climax at the MIDDLE** → Falling action → Catastrophe/denouement.
The detail most summaries get wrong: climax sits mid-story, not near the end. Macbeth kills the king at the midpoint; everything after is unraveling.
**Best for:** Downfall and consequences arcs. **Wrong shape for:** commercial fiction holding its climax to the final act.

### 3. Hero's Journey (Campbell/Vogler) — transformation engine
**12 stages:** Ordinary World → Call to Adventure → Refusal → Mentor → Crossing the Threshold → Tests/Allies/Enemies → Approach to the Inmost Cave → **Ordeal** → Reward → Road Back → Resurrection (final proof of change) → Return with the Elixir.
**Best for:** Coming-of-age, adventure, anyone who must *become someone new*. **Weakness:** forcing stages the story doesn't need — no mentor? Don't invent one because Campbell says so.

### 4. Save the Cat Beat Sheet (Blake Snyder) — the concrete roadmap
15 beats with page targets for a ~110-page script: Opening Image (p1) → Theme Stated (p5) → Setup (p1–10) → Catalyst (p12) → Debate (p12–25) → Break into Two (p25) → B Story (p30) → Fun and Games (p30–55, "the promise of the premise") → Midpoint (p55, false victory/defeat) → Bad Guys Close In (p55–75) → All Is Lost (p75) → Dark Night of the Soul (p75–85) → Break into Three (p85) → Finale (p85–110) → Final Image (p110, mirrors the opening).
**Best for:** Commercial film and genre novels — solves the vague-Act-Two problem with named beats. **Weakness:** rigid page targets produce predictable scripts; scaffolding, not a cage.

### 5. Dan Harmon's Story Circle — TV-sized monomyth
8 steps: You (comfort zone) → Need → Go (unfamiliar situation) → Search (adapt) → Find (get what they wanted) → Take (**pay a heavy price**) → Return → Change.
The circle's top half = known world, bottom half = unknown; steps mirror each other (1↔5, 3↔7).
**Best for:** Episodes, short fiction, individual character arcs inside ensembles — run one circle per major character. Scales down to 22 minutes.

### 6. Seven-Point Structure (Dan Wells) — plot backwards
Hook → Plot Turn 1 → Pinch 1 (antagonist shows strength) → Midpoint (reaction → action) → Pinch 2 (plan collapses) → Plot Turn 2 (final piece gained) → Resolution.
**The trick is the plotting order:** start with the Resolution, write the Hook as its exact inversion (ends confident → starts fearful), place the Midpoint as the hinge, then fill turns and pinches around it.
**Best for:** Mysteries and anyone who knows their ending but not their middle.

### 7. Fichtean Curve — crisis chain
Open mid-action, climb through a series of crises (each bigger than the last) with tiny recoveries between; climax lands ~90% through; brief falling action closes. Exposition gets smuggled into gaps between crises while the reader is already invested.
**Best for:** Thrillers, page-turners, multi-POV novels ending chapters on cliffhangers. **Warning:** run every scene at crisis pitch and the reader goes numb — partial recoveries are what make the next spike register.

### 8. Kishōtenketsu — structure without conflict
Four parts from East Asian tradition: **Ki** (introduce characters/setting) → **Sho** (deepen them, build pattern) → **Ten** (a turn: contrast or new angle that changes the frame — not necessarily a twist or a fight) → **Ketsu** (connect the turn back to Ki/Sho, recontextualizing everything).
**Best for:** Quiet, literary, observational stories that feel distorted by rising-conflict models (much Ghibli energy lives here). **Weakness:** the turn feels random when Ketsu fails to connect it.

## Choosing — quick heuristics

```
Character transformation story?      → Hero's Journey / Story Circle
Commercial genre film?               → Save the Cat
Episode or short story?              → Story Circle
Know the ending, not the middle?     → Seven-Point (plot in reverse)
Tragedy / downfall arc?              → Freytag's Pyramid
Thriller pacing?                     → Fichtean Curve
Quiet, contrast-led narrative?       → Kishōtenketsu (or loose three-act)
Not sure?                            → Three-act as skeleton, borrow beats from others
```

Diagnosis trick: when a draft feels wrong, map it against two structures. Sagging middle? Check which Save the Cat beat is missing. Hollow arc? Run the Story Circle and find the missing step.

## Scene-Level Tools (structures tell you WHERE; these tell you HOW)

- **Scene = goal → conflict → disaster; Sequel = reaction → dilemma → decision.** Alternate them.
- **Show don't tell:** render emotion through action and sensory detail ("her thumb rubbed the ring's edge" > "she was nervous").
- **Dialogue does two jobs minimum:** reveal character AND advance plot/information. Small talk that does neither gets cut.
- **Enter late, leave early** — start scenes at the last possible moment, cut at the first.
- **Stakes escalate in cycles,** they don't just grow: progress → cost → bigger problem → recovery → bigger problem.
