# History

> **Created:** 2026-10-17
> **Part of:** [[00_domain-of-knowledge-overview]] (the crosswalk index) and [[07_kinds-of-knowledge]] (the map theory).
> **The question:** What is history as a domain of knowledge, where do the maps place it, and what does that placement tell us about how it is learned?

## TL;DR

History studies the human past as evidence: what happened, and how we know. Aristotle ranked it below science, because history only records particulars, and even below poetry, which at least reaches the universal; that old slight is actually a precise diagnosis of the method. The historian's deed is arguing from sources: weighing evidence, and defending an interpretation that others can attack. Nothing here can be rerun in a lab, so the domain's critical method keeps it scientific in spirit while its narrative form keeps it literary. Its placement follows: its own area in the IB Theory of Knowledge guide, humanities for the OECD, and (oddly) a strand inside สังคมศึกษา (Social Studies) in Thai basic education.

## 1. What the domain studies

History studies change over time in human affairs: events, structures, lives, and the traces they left. Its characteristic question is double-barreled and inseparable: "**what happened, and how do we know?**" The second half is what makes history a discipline rather than storytelling. Any claim about the past must stand on sources: documents, material remains, testimonies, records, and an explicit argument for why those sources should be believed.

What counts as progress is interpretive: newly read or newly found evidence, an interpretation that survives criticism, a question reframed so that old evidence speaks again. Every claim is about particulars, about specific people and singular events, and that is exactly what Aristotle held against it. Generality, if it comes at all, comes by comparison and synthesis, never by law.

The domain therefore lives on a seam that runs through its own method: **science in its criticism** (sources are tested, claims are falsifiable, honesty about uncertainty) and **literature in its exposition** (the past must be narrated to be understood at all). Every historian negotiates that seam daily.

Aristotle's cell is the most interesting, because the slight is a definition. History records particulars, and for Aristotle knowledge proper dealt in the universal: poetry, which invents typical characters, ranked higher than history, which recounts what Alcibiades actually did. Notice what the ranking concedes: history's fidelity to the singular event is exactly its method, and it is precisely what makes the past unrepeatably knowable. The domain has spent two millennia turning that supposed weakness into its critical strength.

The rest of the maps promote it. The IB Theory of Knowledge guide gives history its own area, one of five, on par with the natural sciences and mathematics. UNESCO gives it its own field (55). Only the Thai curriculum files it inside สังคมศึกษา (Social Studies), with civics and geography for company, which tells you something about what a school system wants a single subject to carry. A domain's address in a map says as much about the map as about the domain.

## 3. Its method and culture

How claims earn trust here: **sources, evidence, interpretation**. A historical claim earns trust when its sources are identified, tested for provenance and bias, and argued into a conclusion that a skeptic can check and attack. This is the interpretive-humanistic method family at its most disciplined: no controlled experiment is possible, because the past cannot be rerun. The community's error-correction is slow, adversarial, and permanent: footnote by footnote, a better-sourced argument overturns a weaker one, and the archive remains open for any challenger.

In Biglan's terms history is **soft-pure**: divergent in interpretation, particularistic in subject, aimed at understanding rather than application. A good day in the domain: you are in an archive or a corpus of sources, you find a passage that quietly breaks someone else's argument, and you write the paragraph that makes it count. Evidence constrains interpretation tightly but rarely decides it, which is why honest historians state their uncertainty instead of hiding it.

## 4. Its seams and neighbors

- [[14_anthropology]]: history and anthropology share the past and the interpretive method. Archaeology sits on the border: material sources for one, fieldwork culture for the other.
- [[18_literature]]: the narrative seam. History must tell stories to explain, and the line between evidence-backed narration and literary invention is the domain's most anxious border.
- [[13_political-science]]: history particularizes power and the state; political science generalizes from the same events. One gives the case, the other the pattern.

## 5. How to learn this domain

> Status: STUB. The full treatment comes in the meta-learning note on learning each kind of knowledge (question 2).

The domain's practice looks like its practitioners' deed: **arguing from sources**. You learn history the way historians work: pick a question, go to the primary sources, weigh them against each other, and write the argument with every claim tied to evidence someone else can check. Reading histories teaches standards and shapes; the skill itself is built in the handling of sources, because the knowing-how of the domain is exactly the weighing and the arguing. The calm expression of uncertainty that [[06_disagreeing-without-rage]] describes is, in this domain, a professional virtue.

## Key takeaways

- History studies the human past as evidence, and every claim answers two questions: what happened, and how do we know?
- Aristotle's "below science" ranking is a diagnosis of the method: particulars, never laws, which is exactly why the past is knowable at all.
- The method is scientific in criticism and literary in narration, and the seam between them runs through every historian's desk.
- The past cannot be rerun in a lab: trust comes from sources tested and arguments that survive adversarial reading.
- To learn it is to do it: the historian's deed is arguing from sources, so handle evidence before you narrate.
