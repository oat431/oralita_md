# Geography

> **Created:** 2026-10-17
> **Part of:** [[00_domain-of-knowledge-overview]] (the crosswalk index) and [[07_kinds-of-knowledge]] (the map theory).
> **The question:** What is geography as a domain of knowledge, where do the maps place it, and what does that placement tell us about how it is learned?

## TL;DR

Geography studies the Earth's surface as the human home: where things are, why they are there, and how places shape lives. It is the double-homed domain: physical geography and human geography sit in different faculties, and the maps disagree about which family owns the whole. The OECD files only the human half (Social sciences 5.7) among the social sciences, while physical geography drifts toward earth science and UNESCO gives Geography its own field (54). Its method joins two crafts: maps, meaning representation and spatial analysis, and fieldwork, meaning being on the ground. What the placement tells the learner: geography is learned by reading landscape and building maps, and its two halves may need two different learning paths.

## 1. What the domain studies

Geography studies the arrangement of things across the face of the Earth and the reasons for that arrangement. Its characteristic question is deceptively small: "**why here?**" Why do cities grow at river mouths? Why does this valley speak a different dialect than the next one? Where will a flood, a factory, or a migration land, and on whom?

The domain is famously split. Physical geography studies landforms, climate, water, soils, and the processes that shape the surface. Human geography studies population, settlement, economic activity, borders, and the meanings people attach to places. Both halves claim the whole word "geography", which is why universities house them in different departments and why every map below has to choose a drawer.

Progress in geography looks like better maps (literal and analytic), sharper spatial models, and places made newly legible: a flood risk understood, a migration explained, a city's growth pattern traced. The discipline's oldest deliverable is still its best: a map that lets a stranger see the pattern you found.

## 2. Who claims it

| Map | What it calls it / where it sits |
|---|---|
| Aristotle, c. 350 BCE | theoretical (part of natural philosophy) |
| IB Theory of Knowledge, 2022 | Human sciences |
| OECD Frascati, 2007 | Social sciences 5.7 (social and economic geography: only the human half!) |
| Thai Basic Education, 2551 | สังคมศึกษา (Social Studies) |
| Dewey Decimal | 910 |
| ISCED-F 2013 | Social sciences (03), with physical geography leaning toward Natural sciences, mathematics and statistics (05) |
| UNESCO nomenclature, 1973 | Geography 54 (its own field) |

The Frascati cell is doing the most interesting work: it files "social and economic geography" among the social sciences and, as the cell itself admits, that covers **only the human half!** Physical geography's subject matter (climate, landforms, water) is claimed by the natural sciences instead. The ISCED row says the same thing more diplomatically: one domain, two broad fields, with the border running right through the departmental wall between the geography and earth science buildings.

Aristotle's placement is the oldest trace of the split: geography belonged to theoretical natural philosophy, because studying the Earth was studying nature. When the human half matured, the domain outgrew any single family and UNESCO promoted it to its own field (54). Dewey's 910 is gentler: geography gets a general class near travel and regional description, which flatters the map-making half while the physical processes half lives near the earth sciences.

## 3. Its method and culture

How claims earn trust here: **maps and fieldwork**. A geographic claim is trusted when the spatial evidence holds up: measurements, transects, remote sensing, survey data, and a map that reproduces the pattern for anyone who looks. But the interpretive half earns trust like the social sciences do: by evidence gathered in places, argued into an account of why this place is like this. The method family is empirical, and the domain is honest about being two of them at once.

In Biglan's terms geography straddles the grid: physical geography is **hard-applied** (convergent, aimed at predicting and managing natural processes), human geography is **soft-pure to soft-applied** (divergent, aimed at understanding places and people). A good day in the domain: you are outdoors with an instrument or a notebook, you come back with measurements or fieldnotes, and a map you make reveals a pattern you did not expect. Error-correction is unusually concrete: the river is not where you drew it, the transect disagrees with your model, and the ground wins the argument.

## 4. Its seams and neighbors

- [[05_earth-and-space-sciences]]: the climate and physical-geography seam. When geography models landforms and climate systems, whose knowledge is the model? The method is shared and the faculties are not.
- [[12_sociology-and-demography]]: population, settlement, and economic geography study the same patterns sociology and demography do, from the direction of place.
- [[16_history]]: historical geography and the geography of history: every historical event happens somewhere, and places have biographies of their own.

## 5. How to learn this domain

> Status: STUB. The full treatment comes in the meta-learning note on learning each kind of knowledge (question 2).

The domain's practice looks like its practitioners' deed: **making maps from ground truth**. You learn geography by walking transects, reading landscapes, collecting spatial data, and building the map that shows the pattern yourself, because a map read is far weaker than a map made. Field notebooks and GIS exercises are this domain's version of problem sets. And because the domain is double, expect two learning paths: process and measurement for the physical half, place and interpretation for the human half.

## Key takeaways

- Geography studies the Earth's surface as the human home, and its question is the small, sharp one: why here?
- It is the double-homed domain: physical and human geography sit in different families on every map, and the OECD cell admits it files only the human half.
- Aristotle filed it under natural philosophy; UNESCO promoted it to its own field (54); both moves describe the domain's split.
- Maps and fieldwork are the method: claims are checked against ground truth, and the ground wins arguments.
- To learn it is to do it: walk, measure, notebook, map.
