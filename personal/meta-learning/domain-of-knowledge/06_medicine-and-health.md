# Medicine and Health

> **Created:** 2026-10-17
> **Part of:** [[00_domain-of-knowledge-overview]] (the crosswalk index) and [[07_kinds-of-knowledge]] (the map theory).
> **The question:** What is medicine and health as a domain of knowledge, where do the maps place it, and what does that placement tell us about how it is learned?

## TL;DR

Medicine studies health and disease and the practices that restore and protect health: knowledge in the service of a patient. Its method is triple: empirical biomedicine for causes, clinical design for what to do, and normative care for what should be done. Aristotle files it as **productive**, not theoretical: medicine as techne, knowledge whose excellence is visible in its results. No map denies it a home, but no two maps agree on what kind of home it is (Section 2), and its learning culture, apprenticeship, is the oldest clue to what the domain actually is.

## 1. What the domain studies

Medicine studies the human being in health and disease: how the body works, how it fails, which interventions help, and how care should be given. Its characteristic questions are diagnostic, practical, and moral at once: "what is wrong with this patient?", "what does the evidence say works here?", and "what is the right thing to do for this person?"

What counts as progress: a treatment that works, a mechanism clarified, a disease prevented, a guideline changed when the evidence changes it. The unit of the domain's knowledge is the patient, and the patient is not a laboratory system: variation, context, and meaning all matter, and "health" itself is partly a normative concept, which is why [[21_ethics]] is a neighbor rather than a stranger. The domain's great institutional achievement is evidence-based medicine: the habit of judging interventions by trials and outcomes rather than by authority, elegance, or tradition.

## 2. Who claims it

| Map | What it calls it / where it sits |
|---|---|
| Aristotle, c. 350 BCE | productive (medicine as techne) |
| IB Theory of Knowledge, 2022 | Natural sciences and human sciences (application; no separate area) |
| OECD Frascati, 2007 | Medical and health sciences 3 |
| Thai Basic Education, 2551 | สุขศึกษาและพลศึกษา (Health and Physical Education) |
| Dewey Decimal | 610 |
| ISCED-F 2013 | Health and welfare (09) |
| UNESCO nomenclature, 1973 | Medical Sciences 32 |

This is the crosswalk's most crowded disagreement, and the Aristotle row sets it up: **productive (medicine as techne)**. In his scheme medicine was not theoretical knowledge of nature but productive knowledge of how to make something (here, health) and judged by outcomes. Every later map answers that framing differently. UNESCO files Medical Sciences 32 in the applied block right beside Agricultural Sciences 31. Frascati gives it a whole numbered field, 3, as a research area. TOK refuses it an area at all, calling it an application of both natural and human sciences: medicine draws its mechanisms from one and its understanding of persons from the other. The Thai curriculum files it under Health and Physical Education, a school learning area that names practice and welfare rather than a science.

The pattern to notice: the more a map is about **research**, the more medicine counts as knowledge in its own right; the more a map is about **school subjects or ways of knowing**, the more medicine counts as application. That gradient is the map's implicit answer to what kind of knowledge practice is.

## 3. Its method and culture

How claims earn trust here: the triple method. Empirical biomedicine runs the trials and the cohorts to find what works and why. Clinical design is the craft of diagnosis and treatment: taking a body of evidence and a particular patient and constructing what to do. Normative care holds the whole together: consent, welfare, the duty owed to the person in the bed. A claim in medicine is trusted when the evidence is strong, the reasoning fits the case, and the practice is defensible to the patient.

In Biglan's terms medicine is **hard-applied**: convergent methods, high consensus on fundamentals, and the aim is not understanding but a result in the world. Its learning culture is famously apprenticeship: the aphorism "see one, do one, teach one" describes the actual transmission of clinical **knowing-how**, which no textbook contains (the feel of an abdomen, the timing of a decision, the conversation that secures trust). Error-correction is institutionalized through evidence-based medicine: guidelines change when trials say so, and the domain treats its own past confidence as provisional. Morbidity and mortality conferences are [[04_logical-fallacies]] and [[06_disagreeing-without-rage]] practiced as professional duty: dissecting what went wrong, without theater.

A good day in the domain: a diagnosis clicks before it is too late, a patient improves, and the reasoning survives the next morning's scrutiny.

## 4. Its seams and neighbors

- [[04_life-sciences]]: the mechanism seam. Medicine applies biology's explanations to patients: where application begins is where the deeds diverge.
- [[21_ethics]]: the normative seam. Consent, resource allocation, end-of-life care: the questions where evidence underdetermines the answer and the domain must still act.
- [[08_engineering-and-technology]]: the design seam. Devices, imaging, prosthetics: medicine as a design domain builds interventions, and its engineering cousin builds the tools.

## 5. How to learn this domain

> Status: STUB. The full treatment comes in the meta-learning note on learning each kind of knowledge (question 2).

The practitioner's deed is **caring for a patient**, so the practice that builds the domain is apprenticeship: see one, do one, teach one, in that order and under supervision. The domain's knowledge is half propositional and half skilled performance, which is why medical education pairs years of study with years of practice and calls both essential. Evidence-based medicine then supplies the error-correction habit: trust the trial over the anecdote, including your own anecdote.

## Key takeaways

- Medicine is knowledge in service of a patient: empirical, design, and normative methods in one domain.
- Aristotle's "productive (medicine as techne)" frames the argument every map answers differently: research maps grant it a field, school and knowledge maps call it application.
- Biglan hard-applied, with apprenticeship as the transmission belt for the skilled half of its knowledge.
- Evidence-based medicine is the domain's error-correction culture: guidelines change when the evidence changes.
- To learn it is to do it: see one, do one, teach one, and let the trials overrule your favorite anecdote.
