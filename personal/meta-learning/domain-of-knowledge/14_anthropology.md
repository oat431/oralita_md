# Anthropology

> **Created:** 2026-10-17
> **Part of:** [[00_domain-of-knowledge-overview]] (the crosswalk index) and [[07_kinds-of-knowledge]] (the map theory).
> **The question:** What is anthropology as a domain of knowledge, where do the maps place it, and what does that placement tell us about how it is learned?

## TL;DR

Anthropology studies the human species whole: our bodies, our cultures, our languages, and our pasts as one connected problem. Its identity is famously double: biological anthropology leans toward the natural sciences, cultural anthropology toward interpretation and the humanities. The split is visible on every map: the OECD files it under Social sciences 5.9 (other social sciences), while the IB Theory of Knowledge guide argues with itself over whether anthropology belongs among the sciences or the humanities. Its method is equally distinctive: ethnography and fieldwork, the long practice of living among the people you study. What the placement tells the learner: this is a domain you learn by going, staying, and listening.

## 1. What the domain studies

Anthropology asks what a human being is, taken whole. Its traditional subfields split the question: cultural anthropology studies how people make meaning and organize life together; biological (physical) anthropology studies human bodies, evolution, and variation; linguistic anthropology studies how language shapes social life; archaeology studies past peoples through what they left behind. The characteristic question is not "what do humans generally do?" but "**what do these humans do, here, and why does it make sense to them?**"

Progress in anthropology looks like richer comparison and sharper interpretation: a monograph that makes a strange practice legible, a find that revises the human timeline, a concept (culture, reciprocity, kinship) that other domains borrow. The price of the method is scope: ethnography gives depth in one place rather than representativeness across all places.

The double identity is the domain's permanent condition. Biological anthropology runs labs, measures bones, and argues with evolutionary biology; cultural anthropology writes fieldnotes and argues with sociology and philosophy. Both call themselves anthropology, and the crosswalk below has to file the whole family somewhere.

## 2. Who claims it

| Map | What it calls it / where it sits |
|---|---|
| Aristotle, c. 350 BCE | NO HOME (dashes) |
| IB Theory of Knowledge, 2022 | Human sciences |
| OECD Frascati, 2007 | Social sciences 5.9 (other social sciences) |
| Thai Basic Education, 2551 | สังคมศึกษา (Social Studies) |
| Dewey Decimal | 300s (social sciences) |
| ISCED-F 2013 | Social sciences (03) |
| UNESCO nomenclature, 1973 | Anthropology 51 |

The most interesting cell is Aristotle's: anthropology has **no home** in the oldest map, because the question "what is a human being?" was absorbed into natural philosophy, ethics, and rhetoric rather than given a discipline of its own. Anthropology as a named field is modern, and every map has had to find it a drawer.

The IB Theory of Knowledge guide's own border dispute is the second tell. It places anthropology among the human sciences, yet the guide keeps having to explain whether the domain's scientific halves (biological anthropology, archaeology) belong there too. When a map argues with itself about a domain, the domain is genuinely double. UNESCO's answer is the sharpest: it gives Anthropology its own field (51), separate from sociology and from the natural sciences. A domain's promotion to its own field says as much about the map's wish for tidy drawers as about the domain.

## 3. Its method and culture

How claims earn trust here: **fieldwork and ethnography**, plus the archaeologist's excavation and the biologist's lab where the subfield demands it. The ethnographic claim "this is how these people live and why" is backed by months of residence, participation, recorded interviews, and fieldnotes other researchers can question. Interpretation is checked against evidence, but the evidence is thick description rather than controlled experiment: a soft, interpretive-empirical method family, closer to history and sociology than to physics.

In Biglan's terms anthropology is mostly **soft-pure**: divergent, particularistic, aimed at understanding people in context rather than controlling variables. Biological anthropology's lab half is harder and more applied, which is exactly why the classification argument never quite ends. A good day in the domain: you are somewhere strange to you, you ask a question that embarrasses you slightly, and a person answers it at length. Error-correction is real but slow: the community reads your fieldnotes, your informants' testimony, your comparisons; bad ethnography is caught years later by a better ethnographer.

## 4. Its seams and neighbors

- [[12_sociology-and-demography]]: the closest sibling. Both study people in groups; sociology tends toward surveys and generalization, anthropology toward residence and particular cases. The border is more diplomatic than real.
- [[04_life-sciences]]: biological anthropology's seam. When anthropology studies human evolution and variation, is it biology with fossils or culture with bones? The method moves between the two.
- [[17_language-and-linguistics]]: linguistic anthropology studies language as social life; linguistics studies language as structure. The same recordings, two very different questions.

## 5. How to learn this domain

> Status: STUB. The full treatment comes in the meta-learning note on learning each kind of knowledge (question 2).

The domain's practice looks like its practitioners' deed: **fieldwork**. You learn anthropology the way an anthropologist does it: pick a community or a question, spend time there, write fieldnotes every day, and try to make one strange practice legible to someone outside it. Reading monographs teaches the craft's standards, but the knowing-how lives in the asking, the listening, and the revision of your own first impressions. The calibration of [[05_dunning-kruger-effect]] applies sharply here: your first month of fieldwork produces confident theories that your third month dismantles.

## Key takeaways

- Anthropology studies the human species whole, and its double identity (biological halves lean natural, cultural halves interpretive) never fully resolves.
- Aristotle's map has no home for it: it is a modern field, and every classification has had to find it a drawer.
- Fieldwork and ethnography are the method: trust is earned by residence, participation, and thick description.
- The IB Theory of Knowledge guide's own border dispute (sciences or humanities?) is evidence about the domain, not a flaw in the guide.
- To learn it is to do it: go, stay, listen, write fieldnotes.
